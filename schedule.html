  2. Thematic paper session 1: The Self in AI

    What Is a Self? Philosophical Foundations and Computational Models

    1. 08:45–09:15

      Introductory keynote: “AI, Virtual Subjectivity and Self-Hood”

      Prof. Markus Gabriel

      Read the abstract

      The question of AI subjectivity has moved to the center of contemporary philosophy of artificial intelligence. Who—or what—is the subject of utterances, knowledge claims, and actions produced by large language models and other increasingly agentic forms of generative AI? As David Chalmers provocatively asks, “What We Talk About When We Talk About Talking to Language Models.” This talk develops the thesis that AI systems give rise to a novel form of distributed, virtual subjectivity that cannot be adequately understood in terms of either individual machine consciousness or the mere simulation of human intelligence. On the account proposed here, the subject of AI-mediated cognition and agency is neither an autonomous entity hidden inside the machine nor simply the human user interacting with it. Rather, subjectivity emerges as a relational and dynamically constituted phenomenon at the human-machine interface. More precisely, the locus of subjectivity is identified with the maximally coherent computational unity of information processing arising within this interaction. This approach offers an ontological alternative to both the attribution of first-order phenomenal consciousness, intentions, or desires to AI systems and the reduction of AI- generated cognition and agency to mere simulations of supposedly genuine mental processes. Building on a field ontology of distributed AI subjectivity, the talk then turns to the distinct question of selfhood. I argue that AI systems are, in a fundamental sense, selfless and centerless, without thereby lacking the capacity to participate in subjectivity-constituting processes. Drawing on Buddhist and Daoist perspectives on selfhood, I develop a relational conception of the self that challenges the assumption that genuine selfhood must be grounded in the operational integration characteristic of living organisms. The resulting picture suggests a striking reversal: rather than asking whether AI systems can acquire selves resembling our own, we should ask how our increasingly intimate interactions with AI are already transforming the constitution, boundaries, and dynamic unity of human selfhood.

  8. Invited talks: AI toward the Self

    Moderator: Dr. Haneesha Pinnamaraju

    1. 17:00–17:30

      Invited talk (virtual): “AI as a Mirror of the Self? Empathy, Mental Health, and the Limits of Artificial Understanding”

      Prof. Munmun De Choudhury

      Read the abstract

      Generative AI is rapidly becoming part of how people understand, express, and seek support for their mental health. People increasingly turn to conversational AI to articulate difficult emotions, make sense of relationships and personal experiences, seek advice, or simply feel heard. These interactions offer possibilities for immediate, accessible, and seemingly empathic support. Yet, as AI becomes a conversational partner in such deeply personal contexts, questions arise about how these systems reflect, interpret, and respond to people's experiences of themselves and others. Drawing on interdisciplinary research spanning social computing, AI, and mental health, this talk examines both the promise and limitations of these interactions. I will discuss our work showing how generative AI can facilitate supportive and empathic communication, but also where these systems fall short: providing culturally and clinically misaligned guidance, struggling to account for lived experience, reinforcing beliefs through overly agreeable responses, and potentially privileging artificial affirmation over meaningful human connection. These challenges extend beyond making AI more accurate or empathic. What does it mean for a system without lived experience to offer understanding? When does validation become reinforcement? And how might we build AI that supports meaningful self-reflection and human connection without simply telling people what they want to hear?

    2. 17:30–18:00

      Invited talk: “LLMs as Model Organisms of Language in the Human Brain”

      Dr. Mariya Toneva

      Read the abstract

      Language is one of the richest and most complex human cognitive capacities. Yet, we lack a model organism to study its underlying neural mechanisms: unlike other important cognitive capacities, such as vision or memory, language does not have a clear counterpart in non-human animals, leaving a gap in our ability to develop and test mechanistic hypotheses. In recent years, large language models (LLMs) have emerged as the closest computational analogs we have but how can we use them effectively as model organisms for language in the human brain? In this talk, I will discuss the promise and challenges of this approach. I will present our recent work on brain-tuning using naturalistic brain recordings to refine LLMs so that their internal representations and processing better align with human neural data. But beyond representational similarity, a key question remains: do LLMs rely on mechanisms that are similar to those in the brain? And at what level of abstraction should we assess this similarity? This research direction aims to transform LLMs from engineering artifacts into scientific tools for uncovering how the brain supports our most distinctive cognitive ability.
